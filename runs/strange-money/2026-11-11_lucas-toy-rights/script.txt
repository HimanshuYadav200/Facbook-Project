In the 1970s, a young filmmaker turned down a raise for the toy rights to his movie. You see, George Lucas had just made a hit, and he could have asked Fox for much more money. Instead, he kept his fee at $150,000. In return, he wanted two things nobody cared about. The sequels. And the merchandise. To Fox, toys from a space movie were almost worthless. So they said yes. Then on May 25, 1977, Star Wars opened. Lines wrapped around the block. Every kid wanted a lightsaber, a robot, a Wookiee. But there was a problem. The toys weren't ready. So that Christmas, Kenner sold an empty box. Inside was a certificate promising four action figures by June 1978. Parents bought it anyway. Over the next decades, Star Wars toys, games and T-shirts earned billions of dollars. And Lucas held the rights. Here's the trick. A movie ends when the credits roll. A toy sells again every birthday and every Christmas. Lucas didn't bet on the film. He bet on the shelf. In 2012, Disney bought his company for $4.05 billion.
